Why the cross is central to the Gospel accounts

The cross is central to the Gospel accounts because Jesus speaks about his death before it happens, connects it to his mission and goes to Jerusalem knowing what awaits him. The New Testament then speaks of his death and resurrection together. Mark gives readers a way to trace that connection through the journey, the crucifixion and the burial. Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians explains why those events belong at the heart of the good news.

Why Mark introduces Jesus’ death before Jerusalem

Read Mark 8:27–38 as a single conversation. Peter identifies Jesus as the Christ. Jesus then tells the disciples that he will suffer, be rejected, be killed and rise. Peter objects. The placement of this exchange helps the reader see what is at stake. The question of who Jesus is leads straight into the question of what his mission involves. Peter’s response shows that the connection takes time to understand.

Notice how much information Mark gives the reader at this point. The execution itself is still ahead, and Jesus has already spoken of rejection and resurrection. When you arrive at the account of his death, you have earlier words to read alongside it. One way to follow the thread is to mark every reference to suffering, death or rising in chapter 8 and then look for those same ideas on the road in chapter 10.

How Jesus describes his purpose

In Mark 10:32–45, Jesus leads the way toward Jerusalem and again tells the Twelve what will happen to him. James and John then ask for places beside him in glory. Jesus answers by speaking about service. At the end of his response he describes the Son of Man as coming to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many.

“Ransom” is a word associated with release. Here the phrase gives readers a reason to ask what Jesus understands his death to accomplish for others. It comes in a conversation about power, where the brothers are asking what they might receive and Jesus speaks of what he gives. Keep the sentence in that setting when you read it. Mark places the explanation on the road, so the reader can carry it into the later account of the crucifixion.

What Mark asks readers to notice at the cross

Mark 15 moves from Jesus before Pilate to the crucifixion, death and burial. The accusation above Jesus identifies him as king. Earlier, soldiers had mocked him using royal language. Near the end of the scene, a centurion who witnesses Jesus’ death calls him the Son of God. Read those details in sequence. They return the reader to the question Jesus asked on the road about his identity.

Mark also names women who watch from a distance. Later in the chapter, Joseph of Arimathea asks Pilate for Jesus’ body and places it in a tomb. Two women see where he is laid. The last lines of the chapter give the account a location and identify people who observe what happens. Those details are worth reading at the same pace as the preceding scene, particularly if you usually move quickly from the cross to the next chapter.

Why Paul reads death and resurrection together

In 1 Corinthians 15:1–8, Paul reminds the church of the message he received and passed on. He describes Christ’s death as being for our sins, then mentions burial, resurrection and appearances. That compact sequence helps answer a reader’s question about why the cross matters. Paul gives the death a purpose in relation to sin and places it within a message that includes resurrection.

The letter’s argument continues in verses 12–20. Paul asks what would follow if the dead were never raised. He ties the answer to what Christians believe about Christ and to the substance of their faith. Reading that argument alongside the opening lines of the chapter shows how much rests on the connection between the death and the resurrection. Mark tells the events as a story, while Paul recalls them in a letter to address a question in the church.

A reading exercise for the three passages

Put Mark 10:45, the burial scene in Mark 15 and the opening of 1 Corinthians 15 on the same page of notes. Write one sentence for each passage. In the first, record how Jesus describes the purpose of giving his life. In the second, record what the witnesses see. In the third, record how Paul connects the events in the message he received. If you read in a group, have everyone point to the words that support their sentence before discussing what the passages mean together.
