The Good Samaritan: who is my neighbor?

The Good Samaritan answers a question about being a neighbor through an account of mercy in action. At the end, Jesus asks which traveler became a neighbor to the injured man. The answer points to the one who helped him, and Jesus calls His listener to do likewise. Reading the opening conversation with the story makes that ending clearer.

The conversation begins before the road

A legal expert asks Jesus about inheriting eternal life. Jesus directs him to what is written in the law and asks how he reads it. The man answers with love for God and love for one’s neighbor. Jesus affirms the answer. Only then comes the question about who counts as his neighbor, with Luke noting the man’s desire to justify himself. Keep this exchange attached to the parable. Luke 10:25–29

The opening makes the listener’s knowledge part of the encounter. He can state the command, yet the conversation continues into how it bears on another person. As a reading exercise, copy the opening question and leave a space beneath it for the question Jesus asks at the end. Comparing the two will help you notice how the conversation develops.

Follow what the travelers actually do

The story places an injured man on the road from Jerusalem to Jericho. Robbers have left him in desperate condition. A priest and a Levite see him and pass by. A Samaritan sees him and responds with compassion. The narrative gives the reader actions to follow, and those actions form the contrast within the story. Luke 10:30–33

When retelling the passage, distinguish what you read from a motive you imagine. It can be tempting to explain the people who pass by with a detailed account of their schedules, fears or intentions. Luke does not supply those explanations here. You can discuss questions the story raises while keeping your account of their actions faithful to the words on the page.

Try making a short list of verbs for each traveler. That simple exercise gives a group something concrete to compare. Ask which actions are recorded and which details remain unstated. It also helps a child who remembers the names of the characters but has not yet followed the sequence of the encounter.

Mercy includes the next stage of care

The Samaritan approaches, attends to the wounds, transports the injured man and brings him to an inn. The help continues there and includes provision for further care after his departure. The story gives time to these practical steps. Read through them without rushing directly to the final question. Luke 10:34–35

A useful observation is that the traveler’s response develops beyond the moment of seeing. He takes responsibility for a sequence of actions and involves another person in continuing the care. When discussing the passage, identify those steps before moving to your own circumstances. That gives any application a basis in what the story describes.

For a contemporary discussion, choose an ordinary example such as helping someone find the right person to contact when they need assistance. Label it as a discussion example. Ask what attention and follow-through would require in that situation. Let the actual needs of the person guide the conversation, and allow participants to suggest different reasonable ways to help.

Listen carefully to the final question

Jesus asks which of the three became a neighbor to the man who encountered the robbers. The legal expert identifies the one who showed mercy. The command to do likewise follows. Compare that exchange with the earlier question asking who the speaker’s neighbor is. The ending directs attention to the conduct of the person who responds to need. Luke 10:36–37

This is a good place to pause before summarizing the parable. Write a sentence that includes both mercy and action, then check it against the ending. Does your sentence preserve the relationship between the question and the answer? A summary can be brief while still keeping the part of the conversation that gives the story its direction.

Questions for reading together

Give each participant the complete passage, Luke 10:25–37. Invite someone to read the opening conversation and someone else to read the story and conclusion. Allow a quiet moment for everyone to mark an action or a question. Begin discussion with what each person can point to in the text.

Then ask how the final instruction addresses the listener who began the conversation. People may notice different details, so keep the passage open while comparing answers. If a question depends on information outside the passage, write it down for further study. The group can continue with the details it has already established.

A small exercise after reading

Think of an occasion when you noticed someone needing help. Consider what you understood about the need and whether any next step remained unfinished. You can keep the reflection private. Choose one practical action you can responsibly take, and check whether the person actually wants that assistance.
