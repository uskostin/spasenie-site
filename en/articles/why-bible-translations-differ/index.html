Why Bible translations differ and how to compare them

Two Bibles can describe the same event with different English words. Translators must decide how to carry the wording and meaning of an ancient text into another language. Compare a complete paragraph, identify the editions and read their notes before deciding what a difference means. A familiar expression and a clearer modern expression may point to the same thing; other differences need a closer look at the underlying text.

Start with the name of the edition

Before comparing sentences, look at the title page or the version selected in your app. Record the translation and revision. A study Bible adds introductions and commentary to a translation; those notes have their own authors. A devotional book may quote Scripture and then discuss it. A retelling can condense a story. Knowing what you have open prevents a commentary or shortened narrative from being mistaken for the wording of a Bible translation.

The NIV translators’ preface explains their attention to meaning alongside the differences between languages. The ESV preface describes an approach that gives particular attention to the original wording and style. Both discuss the work required to communicate accurately. These descriptions tell you what the translators aimed to do. They do not settle every question about an individual verse.

Why word order and sentence length change

A sentence that follows another language’s grammar closely can be hard to understand in English. Translators decide where to divide a long sentence, how to express an idiom and whether a repeated word needs the same English equivalent in different settings. The NIV preface discusses adjustments to sentence structure; the ESV preface also recognises the choices involved in balancing close correspondence with readable English. Readability and attention to wording are concerns within both approaches.

Imagine comparing instructions translated from a language you know well. Two accurate versions may use different sentence patterns. You would ask whether the action and its purpose survived the translation. Apply that question carefully to a Bible passage too. Different wording is a reason to investigate. It does not by itself show that one version has changed the story or that every translation is equally successful.

A small example: the coin in Luke 15

Open Luke 15:8–10 in the NIV and ESV. Both describe a woman who loses one of her ten silver coins, searches for it and calls others to share her joy when she finds it. Both include a note identifying the Greek coin as a drachma. Here the running text gives an accessible description, while the note preserves the more specific term.

This example gives you a practical comparison method. First establish what happens in both versions. Then identify the exact expression that differs. Finally check the notes. A different verb for searching may leave the action unchanged. A footnote may explain a word that looked unfamiliar. Read verse 10 as well: stopping at the coin would leave out the connection the passage itself makes with joy over a sinner who repents.

Read the footnote before making a large claim

Notes can explain an alternative translation, a unit of measurement or a difference among textual witnesses. These are different kinds of information. The NIV preface explains several types of footnote, including alternatives and places where there is uncertainty about the text. Check which kind you are reading. A note about another possible English rendering does not necessarily mean that a different manuscript is involved.

If a familiar verse number appears in a note rather than in the main text, read the explanation in that edition. You may need a fuller source to understand the evidence behind the decision. Avoid settling the issue from a cropped screenshot containing only the missing line. Record the book, chapter, verse and both edition names so that someone helping you can examine the same material.

Keep headings and explanations in their place

The NIV preface explicitly identifies section headings as additions supplied to help readers. A heading can make an episode easier to find, but its wording should not carry the same weight as the passage underneath. Paragraph breaks and explanatory notes also shape how a page looks. When two pages appear very different, check how much of that difference comes from presentation before comparing the sentences themselves.

A children’s retelling may explain a scene in fewer words, and a commentary may add historical information. Use each for its stated purpose. When asking what a biblical author says, return to the passage in a clearly identified translation. Keep your own summary separate as well. That makes it easier to notice when an explanation includes a detail the passage does not actually give.

Choose a main reading copy and a comparison copy

For regular reading, choose an edition you can follow without stopping at every sentence. Read a complete section in it before opening a second version. When something remains unclear, compare that particular section and its notes. Constant switching can make it harder to follow the argument or conversation. Our guide to starting a Gospel offers a simple way to read an episode and keep questions alongside it.

If you are joining a group, ask which translation it will use so that you can follow the reading. That is a practical question, not a reason to assume that everyone must own the same edition.
