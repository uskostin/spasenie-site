John the Baptist: his role in the Gospels

John the Baptist appears at the beginning of Jesus’ public ministry as a preacher calling people to repentance and as a witness directing them to Jesus. He baptizes in the Jordan and speaks of someone greater who is coming after him. Reading his story helps explain how the Gospels introduce Jesus, why repentance has practical consequences, and how a teacher can welcome his own disciples following someone else. Start with Mark 1:1–11, then compare the fuller conversations in John 1 and Luke 3.

Which John are we reading about?

The name can confuse a first-time reader. John the Baptist is the preacher who baptizes Jesus. In Mark 1:19–20, another John appears with his brother James and their father Zebedee; Jesus calls those brothers as disciples. Keep these people separate when taking notes. The title of the Gospel according to John also should not lead you to treat every mention of John inside that book as its narrator speaking about himself.

“Baptist” identifies the baptizing preacher in this story. A discussion of his role should begin with the Gospel passages themselves. The modern history of Baptist churches and their name is a separate subject, requiring different sources. You can understand the encounter at the Jordan without first knowing the history of a denomination.

Preparing the way in Mark

Mark introduces John through scriptural language about a messenger preparing a road. John then appears in the wilderness, and people come to him confessing sins and receiving baptism. These opening scenes give the reader a setting before Jesus begins preaching in Galilee. The movement of the narrative matters: John announces the coming one, Jesus arrives, and the account follows Jesus into his public ministry.

John describes that coming person as stronger than himself. His comparison of water baptism with baptism in the Holy Spirit appears in Mark 1:7–8. Read those words alongside the next scene, where Jesus is baptized and the Spirit descends. Mark has placed the announcement and the encounter close together, allowing readers to follow the connection directly.

Repentance reaches ordinary decisions

Luke 3:10–14 supplies a practical conversation. People ask John what they should do. He speaks about sharing food and clothing with those who lack them. Tax collectors receive an answer concerning what they collect; soldiers are told to avoid extortion and false accusation and to be content with their pay. Different listeners hear instructions connected to their circumstances.

This makes repentance more concrete than a passing feeling of regret. In this passage it involves the way someone treats a neighbor, handles money, or uses power. For discussion, choose one of the groups and identify the action John addresses. Then ask how the answer relates to his earlier demand for fruit consistent with repentance. Keep the application tied to the actual conduct named in the chapter.

Why Jesus comes to John

Matthew 3:13–17 records a conversation absent from Mark’s shorter account. When Jesus comes for baptism, John initially objects, saying that he needs baptism from Jesus. Jesus asks him to allow it, speaking about fulfilling righteousness. John agrees. The scene then includes the Spirit descending and the heavenly declaration concerning the Son.

The question of why Jesus receives baptism can lead into wider theology. Begin by keeping Matthew’s stated answer in view. John recognizes Jesus’ priority, while Jesus deliberately enters this encounter. Comparing Matthew with Mark helps distinguish a detail one Gospel supplies from a detail both include. It also prevents us from supplying invented dialogue or claiming the writers explain every question a later reader might ask.

A witness who directs attention to Jesus

In John 1:19–34, representatives from Jerusalem ask the baptizing John who he is. He denies being the Christ and describes his work with Isaiah’s image of a voice preparing the way. When Jesus approaches, John identifies him as the Lamb of God and recounts the sign involving the Spirit. The emphasis falls on whom John has been sent to identify.

Later, two of his disciples hear him speak about Jesus and follow Jesus. One is named Andrew. This small scene shows what testimony does within the narrative: someone hears a witness and goes to encounter the person being described. If you are reading the Gospel for the first time, mark the speakers carefully. Questions from the visitors, John’s answers, and the narrator’s explanations serve different functions.

What happens when people go to Jesus?

John 3:22–30 describes concern among John’s disciples that people are going to Jesus. John answers by recalling that he has already distinguished himself from the Christ. He uses a wedding image: the bridegroom’s friend rejoices at the bridegroom’s voice. His response treats Jesus’ growing prominence as a reason for joy.

Read this conversation through its immediate situation before making it a general slogan about self-worth. John is explaining his own assignment and his relationship to Jesus. His disciples have interpreted the movement of people as a problem; he explains why it fits the purpose of his witness. The passage offers a useful question for any discussion of religious teaching: where is the teacher directing the listener’s attention?

A question from prison

Matthew 11:2–6 returns to John after his imprisonment. Through his disciples he asks whether Jesus is the coming one. Jesus sends back an answer referring to what they hear and see. The exchange gives readers another encounter to examine, alongside John’s earlier testimony. Matthew then records Jesus speaking to the crowds about John and his prophetic role.

For a manageable reading session, choose Mark 1 and one of these longer passages rather than reading every reference at once. Write down what John says about himself, what he says about Jesus, and what his listeners do next.
