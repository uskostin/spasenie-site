Why do Baptists baptize by immersion?

The Baptist World Alliance beliefs statement describes believers’ baptism by immersion as a biblical way for someone who has come to personal faith in Jesus Christ to declare discipleship publicly. For the Baptists represented by that statement, going into and coming out of the water gives visible form to a confession of faith. This article explains the statement and its biblical context. It does not set a baptism policy for Spasenie Church or every Baptist congregation.

Immersion matters here because the BWA names it explicitly, and Baptists often read the imagery of burial and new life in Romans 6 alongside the act of baptism. The physical action is connected to a person’s faith and to the church hearing that profession.

What does “immersion” mean?

In ordinary language, immersion means going under the water rather than having water placed only on the head. That simple description answers a practical question, but it does not explain the whole Christian meaning of baptism. The BWA statement holds together three ideas: a person has repented and come to faith in Christ, that person declares discipleship publicly, and the baptism is by immersion. Separating the water from faith or from the church’s shared life would leave out part of the statement.

Other Christian churches baptize in different ways. This page describes a published Baptist position without treating the practices of other Christians as a joke or assuming that every Baptist congregation handles every circumstance identically. A visitor can ask a local church what its own teaching says and how it receives people who were baptized elsewhere.

Why connect going under water with a new life?

In Romans 6:3–4, Paul connects baptism with Christ’s death and burial and then speaks about walking in new life. Colossians 2:12 also places burial and being raised with Christ alongside faith in God’s work. Baptists commonly see immersion as a clear physical image of that movement. Water makes the action visible, while the passages direct attention to Christ and to the life of the person following him.

These passages are worth reading in full. Romans 6 goes on to ask how a person should live; it does not stop with a moment in water. The image therefore should not become a claim that an impressive ceremony alone changes someone’s life. Nor should a reader infer from a short article that every question about baptism has been settled. Ask how a congregation understands the biblical texts and what it teaches about faith, grace, and the life that follows baptism.

Is immersion mentioned in the Baptist statement itself?

Yes. The BWA does not leave the method unstated: it names believers’ baptism “by immersion.” The American Baptist Churches USA identity statement speaks of a redeemed people who follow the Lord in believer’s baptism, without using that page to prescribe the physical arrangement of every service. The two documents have different purposes and wording. It is more accurate to tell readers which one supplies a specific claim than to merge them into an imaginary rulebook for all churches.

The BWA is a worldwide fellowship, and ABCUSA is a particular US Baptist body. Neither document is presented here as Spasenie’s own confession. A local church is the right place to ask who makes decisions about baptism, how it prepares a candidate, and which passages it reads during that process.

Why does personal faith come before the public action?

In the BWA wording, immersion belongs to believers’ baptism: it is a public declaration for people who have repented and come to personal faith. The order matters to the explanation. A parent, friend, or pastor can support someone, but cannot profess that person’s faith for them. Our article on believers’ baptism explores that point; the article on personal faith looks at the language behind it.

Public does not require an elaborate speech. It means that baptism is not merely a private symbol hidden from a congregation. How a person speaks, who is present, and what preparation precedes the day are practical matters to discuss with the actual church. A reader who is still learning can visit and ask questions without making a commitment to be baptized.

What if someone cannot safely be immersed?

A question about mobility, health, or fear of water deserves a careful conversation with the church, not a generic promise from a website. Tell the leaders what makes immersion difficult and ask how they approach accessibility and pastoral care. A world-level beliefs statement names the practice but does not describe every individual situation or authorize this article to decide it for a local congregation.

The same applies if someone was baptized as an infant or in another tradition. That history may matter deeply to the person and their family. Ask the church to explain its reasoning respectfully, including how it understands an earlier baptism. A short answer that dismisses another Christian community will not help someone make a thoughtful decision.

How can you explore this before visiting?

Read the BWA statement’s paragraph on baptism, then read Romans 6:3–4 and its surrounding verses. Write down the question that remains: Is it about the meaning of faith, the physical action, an earlier baptism, or practical access? Bringing a specific question gives a church leader something useful to answer. If you are near Orlando, the first-visit guide gives Spasenie’s address and service time. Worship is mainly in Russian, so ask about the language of a conversation before you travel.

Common questions

Does immersion mean baptism is only about water?

No. The cited BWA statement joins immersion with repentance, personal faith, and a public declaration of discipleship.

Is immersion unique to Baptists?

No. Other Christians also practice immersion. This article explains why the cited Baptist statement names it.

Does this article state how Spasenie baptizes?

No. Ask the congregation directly about its teaching and arrangements.
