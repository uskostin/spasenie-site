What does following Jesus mean?

In Mark’s Gospel, following Jesus means answering his call, learning from him and letting his teaching change how you act. Mark traces that response from the fishermen who leave their nets to the disciples who must rethink what greatness means. Reading those scenes together helps explain why discipleship takes time and why service matters.

Start with the call by the lake

In Mark 1:14–20, Jesus announces God’s kingdom before calling Simon and Andrew. He finds them casting a net. A little farther on, James and John are mending theirs. The call interrupts familiar work. The brothers respond by going with him, and Jesus speaks of what he will make of them. Their formation begins with his initiative.

Pay attention to the next scene in Capernaum. The new disciples go from the shoreline to a place where they hear Jesus teach. Mark later takes the reader into Simon and Andrew’s house, where Jesus meets a person in need. These scenes put some substance into the word “follow.” The disciples spend time with Jesus and see how he responds in settings they already know. The account describes what these men did when called. A reader can start by asking what Jesus teaches in the passage and what response the passage invites in their own circumstances.

Notice what Peter gets right and what he still has to learn

In Mark 8:27–38, Peter identifies Jesus as the Christ. Jesus then teaches that he will suffer, be rejected, die and rise. Peter objects. The sequence matters because a disciple can recognize Jesus and still resist the direction he is taking. Peter’s objection brings that gap into the open.

When you read the scene, pause between Peter’s answer and his objection. What does Peter seem ready to accept? Which part of Jesus’ teaching changes the conversation? Mark records the disagreement without giving a detailed account of Peter’s motives. Keeping those two things separate makes for a better reading. It also gives a group something concrete to discuss without speculating about the character of the person asking a question.

Read the call to take up the cross in context

Jesus addresses the crowd as well as the disciples after speaking about his own suffering. He links self-denial and taking up the cross with following him. The surrounding verses speak of losing one’s life for Jesus and the gospel and ask what a person gains by acquiring the world at the cost of life. That setting gives the image its weight. The issue is allegiance when following Jesus carries a cost.

A reader might consider an ordinary situation where telling the truth would mean admitting an error. The passage can prompt an examination of what the person is trying to protect and why. That example is an application of the reading, and the scene itself remains about Jesus’ call. Use the surrounding verses when discussing the phrase so that the image retains its connection to him and to the gospel.

Watch how Jesus answers a request for status

In Mark 10:32–45, Jesus again tells the disciples what awaits him in Jerusalem. James and John ask for places beside him in glory. The other disciples become indignant. Jesus gathers them and speaks about how rulers exercise power, then describes greatness among his followers in terms of service. He connects that teaching to his own purpose in coming.

Compare James and John here with the two brothers by the boat in chapter 1. They have followed Jesus for part of the story, and their request shows how much they still have to learn. Service becomes a question about how they relate to the people around them. A practical exercise is to think of a shared responsibility at home, at work or in a group. Ask who carries the part that others rarely see and what help that person would actually welcome. The answer may be as specific as taking a turn or listening before offering advice.

Read the three scenes across three sittings

Begin with Mark 1:14–20 and write one sentence about Jesus’ initiative and one about the brothers’ response. Next, read Mark 8:27–38 and note where the conversation turns after Peter’s answer. Finally, read Mark 10:32–45. Put the brothers’ request next to Jesus’ explanation of greatness. You can use the same two questions each day: what does Jesus do or teach here, and how do the people around him respond?

If you read with someone else, give each person a chance to point to a detail before discussing what to do with it. A reader may notice that Jesus addresses the crowd in chapter 8 while another notices the brothers’ request in chapter 10. Their observations can lead to a question that connects the scenes: how does the path Jesus takes shape the life he asks his disciples to lead? The guide to reading Mark places these scenes within the whole book.
