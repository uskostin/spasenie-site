Philip and the Ethiopian official: a conversation about Scripture

In Acts 8:26–40, Philip meets an Ethiopian official reading Isaiah and asks whether he understands the passage. The traveler invites him to explain whom the prophet is describing. Philip begins with that Scripture and tells him the good news about Jesus. The encounter continues to baptism, and the traveler goes on his way rejoicing. The story offers a close look at a conversation that begins with a real question about a text, rather than an imagined question supplied by the person explaining it.

Find the encounter within Acts

Earlier in the chapter, Philip is in Samaria proclaiming Christ. The later scene begins with an instruction from an angel of the Lord to go toward the road from Jerusalem to Gaza. Philip goes, and the narrative introduces the traveler. Reading these transitions helps you follow the change from a public setting to a meeting along the road.

Our guide to reading Acts places these encounters within the book’s wider movement. For this session, begin at verse 26 and read through verse 40. Mark where the setting changes and where someone speaks. You will then have a simple outline of the complete scene before discussing its meaning.

What the narrator tells us about the traveler

The man is described as an Ethiopian eunuch, an official responsible for the treasury of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians. He has been to Jerusalem to worship and is returning in his chariot. He is reading the prophet Isaiah. These are the details Acts supplies to introduce him.

The account does not give his personal name or a full history of how he came to read this book. You can follow his questions without filling those gaps. Retelling his role and what he is doing is enough to establish the scene. If a discussion moves to later traditions about his life, distinguish those traditions from the information in Acts itself.

Philip first hears what he is reading

The Spirit tells Philip to approach the chariot. Philip hears the man reading and asks about his understanding. The question follows attention to what the traveler is already doing. It gives the reader a specific starting point for the exchange.

The man replies that he needs someone to guide him and asks Philip to join him. Notice that the conversation includes an invitation. A practical reflection on this scene can begin with listening carefully and allowing the other person to name their question. That is an application drawn from the sequence of the encounter; it is not a claim that every conversation must reproduce its circumstances.

The question concerns the person in Isaiah’s passage

Acts quotes the passage the official is reading. It speaks about someone led like a sheep to slaughter, remaining silent, and suffering humiliation. The official asks whether the prophet is speaking about himself or someone else. His question identifies the issue he wants explained: whom the words concern.

Read the quotation beside his question. This helps you see why Philip’s answer begins where it does. Instead of assuming the traveler has asked for a survey of every religious subject, follow the problem actually presented in the text. In a group, ask participants to restate the question in their own words, then check that their version preserves its focus.

Philip begins there and speaks about Jesus

Verse 35 summarizes Philip’s answer. Beginning with that Scripture, he tells the official the good news about Jesus. Acts gives the direction of the explanation, while leaving most of the conversation unreported. The narrator’s summary is enough to connect the passage the man reads with the message Philip brings.

It would go beyond this account to publish a reconstructed transcript of Philip’s entire explanation as though Acts supplied it. Further study can compare the passage with other biblical texts, clearly labeled as comparison. Our article on the meaning of Gospel offers a separate introduction to that term; here the focus remains on how this conversation develops from a particular passage.

The traveler asks about baptism

As they continue along the road, they come to water. The official asks what prevents him from being baptized. He orders the chariot to stop, and the account describes Philip baptizing him. The response belongs to the encounter that began with reading and explanation.

Readers comparing editions may notice a difference at verse 37. The linked World English Bible places a confession of faith in a textual note, while the Russian Synodal edition includes it in the main text. Keep the edition visible when quoting or comparing that verse. The sequence of the question about water and the baptism can be followed in both linked editions without silently mixing their wording.

Follow the ending instead of inventing a later biography

After they leave the water, Philip is taken away and the official continues his journey with joy. The final verse follows Philip onward, describing his proclamation through the towns until Caesarea. Acts closes this encounter by showing the two people continuing on different paths.

For a second reading, write down the official’s questions and the invitations or instructions that move the scene forward. Ask what the narrator states directly and what remains unreported. A conversation about Scripture can begin with a modest question and a willingness to read together. If you are just beginning to read the New Testament, the guide for new readers suggests a simple way to record questions and return to the surrounding passage before settling on an answer.
