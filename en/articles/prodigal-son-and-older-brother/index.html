The prodigal son and the older brother

The parable often called the prodigal son continues after the younger son returns. His older brother refuses to enter the celebration, and the father goes out to speak with him. Reading that final conversation is essential to the story’s question about receiving someone who was lost and has been found. Luke 15 begins with a complaint about Jesus welcoming sinners.

Hear the complaint before the parable

At the beginning of the chapter, tax collectors and sinners approach to hear Jesus. Pharisees and scribes complain that He welcomes such people and eats with them. Read this setting before the story of the family. It identifies a question about receiving people and sharing a table that remains relevant when the father calls for a celebration. Luke 15:1–2

The preceding accounts of a lost sheep and a lost coin also include joy over finding what was lost. Read through the chapter and notice the repeated invitation to rejoice. This gives you an observation grounded in the sequence of the stories. It can help you understand why the response to the celebration receives so much attention in the final parable.

Follow the younger son home

The younger son asks for his share, leaves, spends what he has and encounters severe need. He considers his father’s household and decides to return with an acknowledgment of his sin. The narrative then moves from his intention to his actual return. Keep that movement in view when retelling the passage. A decision spoken within the story is followed by action. Luke 15:11–20

For a first reading, make a simple sequence of the events and put the son’s intended words beside the words he later says to his father. Read each in its place. This lets you observe the encounter as it is narrated, including the father’s response. Leave questions beside your notes rather than filling every gap with a proposed motive.

Notice what the father does

The father sees his son while he is still at a distance, responds with compassion and meets him. After the son speaks, the father gives instructions for his reception and a celebration. He explains the joy in terms of a son who was lost and is found. The family relationship remains explicit in the father’s words. Luke 15:20–24

Read the actions and explanation together. The preparations for a meal are part of how the welcome becomes visible. When discussing the scene, identify the words that explain why the father calls for rejoicing. This keeps the discussion tied to the story and gives you a reference to revisit when the older brother challenges the celebration.

Stay for the older brother’s conversation

The older son comes from the field and asks what is happening. When he hears the news, he becomes angry and will not go in. The father comes out and appeals to him. The older son describes his years of service and complains about the reception his brother has received. Listen to how he speaks about himself and about the returning son. Luke 15:25–30

The father answers him as a son, speaks about their continuing life together and refers to the returned man as his brother. Those relationships matter to the exchange. You can compare the language used by the older son and the father without inventing a later argument between them. The response recorded in the passage deserves time of its own.

For discussion, ask someone to read the older brother’s words and another person to read the reply. Then give everyone a moment to identify a detail they noticed. The exercise works best when participants can show the line behind an observation. If two explanations differ, return to the words before deciding whether the difference comes from the text or an assumption.

The ending leaves a response to consider

The father explains why rejoicing is appropriate, and the parable ends with his words. We are not given a narrated decision by the older brother about entering. Allow the ending to remain where Luke places it. A reader can consider the invitation without adding an imagined final scene to the Gospel account. Luke 15:31–32

Return now to the complaint at the start of the chapter. What connection can you see between the objection to welcoming sinners and the objection to the family celebration? Formulate an answer with references to both passages. This comparison helps keep the older brother within the question the chapter has raised from the beginning.

A way to read the whole story together

Read Luke 15:11–32 in one sitting, then return to verses 1–2. Invite each participant to name one action of the father and one question raised by either son. Keep personal reflection voluntary. A person can contribute a careful observation without being asked to identify a family member with a character.
