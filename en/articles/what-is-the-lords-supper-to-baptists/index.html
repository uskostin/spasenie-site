What is the Lord's Supper to Baptists?

The Lord's Supper is a meal Jesus shared with his disciples and asked them to remember. Baptist churches celebrate it with bread and a cup as an act of worship centered on his death, the life of the gathered church, and the hope of his return. If you are visiting a church for the first time, you may hear it called Communion or the breaking of bread.

The Baptist World Alliance beliefs statement names the celebration of the Lord's Supper alongside worship, mission, and baptism as a witness to God's purposes. The American Baptist Churches USA identity statement calls it the meal of the kingdom. Neither sentence tells a visitor exactly when a particular church will celebrate it or who that church will invite to the table.

A meal before it became a church practice

In Luke 22:14–20, Jesus eats with the apostles shortly before his arrest. He takes bread and then a cup, linking the meal with his body and blood and speaking of a new covenant. Reading the scene in its setting matters. These are not stray symbols invented later to make a service feel solemn; they come from a meal Jesus shared with people who knew him.

Paul recalls the bread and cup in 1 Corinthians 11:23–26. His account ties the meal to remembering Jesus and proclaiming his death until he comes. The passage gives the church a way to look back and forward at once. It remembers a specific event, yet refuses to treat the meal as a museum exhibit. Those who gather have to consider what their life together says about the Lord they name.

Why remembrance is more than recalling a fact

People sometimes describe Communion as “just a symbol,” as though the word “remember” means that nothing happens in the room. That understates what Paul's letter asks of the church. He writes to a congregation whose meetings exposed a painful division: some ate well while others were left hungry. He does not answer by asking them to master a private feeling about the bread. He asks them to recognize one another and to wait for one another.

For Baptists, the shared meal directs attention to Christ rather than to the performance of a minister. At the same time, it involves actual neighbors at the table. In a Baptist World Alliance dialogue document, Baptist participants speak about fellowship, care for others, and the meal's orientation toward Christ's coming. That document is an account of a dialogue, not a rule book binding every Baptist church. It is useful because it shows that remembrance, community, and future hope need not compete.

Self-examination without guessing at somebody else's heart

Paul also urges people to examine themselves before eating and drinking. The surrounding verses matter here. His concern is not that a person has found the perfect emotional state. He is confronting a gathering in which the rich humiliated people who had less. A reader may examine not only private thoughts but also how they have treated others and whether there is a relationship that needs attention.

This is a serious invitation, not a license for a visitor to judge who at the table is “worthy.” Nor is it a reason to make an anxious newcomer feel watched. When the church explains the meal, it can speak plainly about Christ and give people space to listen. A visitor who chooses to observe is still present at the worship service. It is better to ask a local church about participation than to infer its policy from somebody else's website.

What can differ between Baptist churches

There is no single schedule for every Baptist congregation. Some celebrate the Supper often; others set aside particular services. Churches may differ in how they distribute the bread and cup, what they say before the meal, and how they describe eligibility for a visiting Christian. The BWA beliefs statement identifies the practice but does not prescribe one timetable or admission rule for all its member churches.

Two simple questions help before a visit: “Is the Lord's Supper part of this Sunday's service?” and “How do you invite guests to take part?” You can also ask whether a family member who prefers to watch may remain seated. Asking is not a test of faith. It prevents an awkward guess during a moment the congregation takes seriously. We do not claim a particular Communion schedule or participation policy for Spasenie here.

Not the same as a shared lunch

Spasenie Church meets in Winter Park in the Orlando area, and a shared lunch normally follows Sunday worship. That hospitality is one part of meeting people. It is not the same as the Lord's Supper described in the New Testament. A newcomer might hear “meal” and reasonably wonder whether the two are connected. The practical distinction is easy: lunch is a time to eat and talk together after the service; the Lord's Supper is a specific act of worship with bread and a cup.

If you would like to know what to expect at the building, read the first-visit guide. If your question is about believer's baptism, that is a different practice and has its own explanation. Neither article should replace a conversation about how this congregation actually arranges its worship.
