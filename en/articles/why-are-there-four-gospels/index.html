Why are there four Gospels?

The New Testament contains four Gospels because it preserves four accounts of Jesus Christ: Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. They concern the same Jesus while telling His story with different openings, selections and arrangements. Reading them alongside one another helps us notice those choices. A claim about exactly why four books entered the collection, however, needs historical evidence beyond the narratives themselves.

Four accounts, rather than four translations

Matthew and Luke are different books. The World English Bible and another English translation of Matthew are different translations of the same book. That distinction matters when someone asks why two Gospel readings sound different. First check whether the passages come from different books or different editions. Our guide to Bible translations addresses the second question.

The word “Gospel” can name the message about Jesus and a book that communicates it. The four books belong to the New Testament, alongside other kinds of writing. Our explanation of the word Gospel sets out that vocabulary. Here the question is how to read the four narratives without losing the shape of each one.

The openings already show different choices

Matthew 1 begins with a genealogy linking Jesus to David and Abraham before telling of His birth. Mark 1 opens with the good news of Jesus Christ and moves to John the Baptist and Jesus’ public ministry. The difference is visible before either book has taken the reader very far.

Luke 1:1–4 addresses Theophilus and explains the preparation and purpose of an account drawing on what had been handed down. John 1:1–18 begins with the Word, creation and the Word becoming flesh. These openings give a reader different starting points for considering who Jesus is.

Write a short description of each beginning before trying to combine them. What names, events or themes does the opening introduce? What does it lead you to expect? This exercise describes what is on the page. It does not require assigning every Gospel a single imagined audience or reducing an entire book to one slogan.

Listen when a writer states a purpose

Luke’s opening gives an explicit reason for writing to Theophilus. Near the end of John 20:30–31, the writer explains that selected signs have been recorded so readers may believe concerning Jesus and have life in His name. John also states that other signs are not written in that book. Selection is therefore part of the account’s own description of itself.

That observation helps with a common reading mistake. If one Gospel includes a detail another does not mention, omission alone does not tell us that the other writer denied it. We still need to read carefully when accounts differ. An explanation should distinguish what the text states from a suggested way of fitting details together.

Compare one shared episode

Try the feeding of the large crowd in Mark 6:30–44 and John 6:1–15. Both accounts describe bread and fish, people eating and food remaining. Read each passage completely before making notes. Then compare the people who speak and the details each narrator chooses to include.

In Mark, the scene follows the apostles’ return and an attempt to withdraw with Jesus. The account connects the crowd’s arrival with Jesus’ response and teaching. John names Philip and Andrew in the discussion about food and identifies the barley loaves associated with a boy. Notice those features without immediately filling the gaps in one account with words taken from the other.

Continue a little beyond the selected passage. Where does the writer take the reader next? A familiar episode can have a different place in the surrounding argument or sequence. Comparison is most useful when it sends you back to the whole book with a better question, rather than producing a composite story that hides both writers’ choices.

What to do with a difficult difference

Write down the exact references and the point that puzzles you. Separate differences in wording from differences in sequence or detail. Check that you have compared complete scenes and the same kind of statement. A heading added by an editor is not part of the narrated conversation, and a translator’s footnote may concern an issue that needs separate investigation.

Some questions require historical or textual research. Claims about dates of composition, which writer used another’s work, or the decisions behind the collection of the four Gospels need their own sources. The passages considered here do not establish all those answers. It is reasonable to keep a question open while reading further, rather than presenting an attractive reconstruction as something explicitly stated in Scripture.

A manageable way to read all four

Read one Gospel consecutively, noting questions and recurring themes. At a suitable point, compare one episode with another Gospel, then return to the first book. You can use two columns for observations and a separate space for unanswered questions. Keep the reference beside each note so you can find the basis for an observation later.

Our first reading guide helps with the smaller task of following a passage in context. If you discuss the books with someone else, choose one passage in advance. Asking why a particular detail matters within that account gives the conversation a clear starting point.
