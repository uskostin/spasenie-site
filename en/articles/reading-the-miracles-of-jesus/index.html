How to read the miracles of Jesus in context

A Gospel miracle story asks readers to notice what Jesus does and what the event reveals about him. The surrounding conversation often supplies the question the event answers. In Mark 2, a healing follows a dispute about forgiveness; in Mark 4, the calming of a storm leaves the disciples asking who Jesus is. John 9 continues long after a man receives sight, following the arguments his healing provokes. Read the complete encounter before deciding what lesson to draw from it.

Begin with the limits of the passage

Choose one account and read the paragraphs immediately before and after it. Note where the scene begins, who is present, and what changes by the end. A printed heading can help you find a passage, but the story may continue beyond that heading. A reply in the next paragraph can explain why the writer included a striking action.

For a first reading, make four brief notes: the situation, the words of Jesus, the response of the people, and the closing question or statement. These notes keep an interpretation anchored to what is on the page. You can add questions in the margin without answering all of them immediately. Our guide to starting a Gospel offers a manageable way to continue reading a whole book.

Mark 2: the question concerns forgiveness

In Mark 2:1–12, a crowd makes it difficult to bring a paralyzed man to Jesus. Four people lower him through the roof. Jesus first speaks about the forgiveness of his sins. Some scribes object inwardly, asking who can forgive sins except God. Jesus addresses their reasoning and connects his command to the man with the authority of the Son of Man to forgive sins.

The man rises, carries his mat, and leaves before everyone. The crowd responds with amazement and praise. Follow that sequence carefully. If a retelling stops at the opening in the roof, it misses the dispute that occupies the center of the scene. The healing is presented in relation to a question about Jesus’ authority.

For discussion, ask which words prompted the objection and how Jesus answers it. Keep the man’s actions at the end beside the claim made earlier. The passage gives readers both something said and something witnessed; reading them together helps explain its structure.

Mark 4: the question remains after the calm

Mark 4:35–41 starts with Jesus proposing a crossing in the evening. A storm threatens the boat while he sleeps. The disciples wake him with a question about whether he cares that they are perishing. Jesus addresses the wind and sea; then he questions their fear and lack of faith. The story ends with their own question about his identity.

Notice that the end is more than relief about improved weather. The disciples are confronted by the person whose command the wind and sea obey. The narrator leaves their question ringing at the close. Read it aloud after the account of the calm, allowing the final sentence to shape your understanding of the whole encounter.

It is reasonable for a reader to think about fear while reading this scene. An application should still preserve the passage’s question about Jesus. Describing a present difficulty as a “storm” is an illustrative comparison. It does not turn the account into a promise that a particular problem will disappear on a particular schedule.

John 9: follow the healed man through the conversations

John 9 begins with the disciples asking about a man blind from birth. Jesus rejects their proposed attribution of his blindness to his own sin or his parents’ sin. The man receives sight, and questions follow from neighbors and religious authorities. His parents are also called. The account gives room to different claims about what happened and what can be concluded from it.

The man reports what he experienced, including the limits of what he knows. Reading these exchanges helps distinguish direct testimony from someone else’s assumption. Mark the questions in the chapter and identify who answers each one. You will see why a summary consisting only of the healing loses much of the story.

Keep the final encounter with Jesus in your reading. John follows the man beyond the examination of his recovery, into a conversation about belief and seeing. A useful discussion question is how his description of Jesus develops as the chapter proceeds.

When the writer calls an event a sign

John 2:1–11 provides another clue about the Gospel’s way of telling these stories. After the wedding at Cana, the narrator describes the event as a beginning of signs and connects it with the revealing of Jesus’ glory and his disciples’ belief. That closing observation gives readers an explicit direction for interpretation.

You need not assign a hidden meaning to every object in a scene. Start with the connection the writer actually makes. Ask what the participants know, what they say, and what the narrator adds for readers. Our overview of John’s Gospel can help place individual encounters within the book’s stated purpose.

Keep observation and application distinct

When a group discusses a miracle story, it helps to separate a sentence beginning “the passage says” from one beginning “this makes me think about.” Both may belong in a conversation. The first should be checkable against the text; the second identifies a reader’s response. This distinction makes disagreement easier to discuss without attributing an interpretation directly to Jesus.

A healing account also gives no basis for diagnosing another person’s illness or blaming them for it. Nor is it a set of treatment instructions. Questions about current symptoms and treatment belong with qualified medical care. A reader can study the Gospel account while taking those practical responsibilities seriously.

For your next session, return to one of the three main passages and write a short summary that includes its final exchange. Ask another reader to show where each sentence comes from. Leave any unanswered question beside the relevant verse, ready to revisit as you continue through the Gospel.
