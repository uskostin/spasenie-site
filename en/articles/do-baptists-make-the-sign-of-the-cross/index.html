Do Baptists make the sign of the cross?

Making the sign of the cross is different from being baptized in water. It is a prayer gesture, and it is not a regular part of worship in many Baptist settings. You should not, however, turn that observation into a rule that no Baptist ever uses it. Published Baptist discussion includes people who choose the gesture. The useful question for a visitor is what the congregation does together and what that action means.

Two meanings that can become confused

In Russian, the word krestit’sya can refer to crossing oneself or to receiving baptism. A conversation can become confusing if one person means the movement of a hand and another means immersion in water. In English the phrases “make the sign of the cross” and “be baptized” separate the actions more clearly. Start by naming which one you want to understand.

The sign of the cross traces the shape of a cross on or over the body. A baptism involves water. For a concrete example from another Christian tradition, the Church of England’s baptismal service contains a direction to mark the candidate’s forehead with a cross. Its presence in that service does not make every use of the gesture a baptism. Context matters when someone describes what they saw.

Why a simple “Baptists never do that” is too broad

In a 2019 Baptist News Global opinion article, Kate Hanch discusses the gesture as a practice unfamiliar to many Baptists and argues for its use. Her contribution is a personal theological argument, not a rule adopted by all Baptist churches. It is evidence that the question is discussed within Baptist life, rather than a practice everyone understands in exactly the same way.

Read the author’s argument at the scale at which it is offered. An opinion column can explain why one person values an action. A congregation’s service instructions explain what that church asks people to do together. Neither should be silently substituted for the other. When comparing churches, identify the source of the explanation before treating it as a statement about a whole tradition.

The cross and the gesture are separate questions

A visitor may notice that people do not cross themselves and wonder whether the cross itself has little significance for the church. That conclusion does not follow. The Baptist World Alliance beliefs statement affirms Christ’s crucifixion and resurrection. It also affirms the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. These convictions concern Christian belief; observing whether someone makes a hand gesture does not establish what that person believes about them.

To understand a congregation’s teaching about Christ, listen to its explanation of the biblical text and read its published statement of belief. Our article on Jesus Christ in Baptist belief introduces that subject. It is a different question from whether a particular movement is used before prayer or at the entrance to a building.

How believers’ baptism differs

The same BWA statement describes believers’ baptism by immersion in connection with repentance and personal faith in Jesus Christ. Its meaning cannot be reduced to crossing oneself before a service. The actions are distinct even where another church includes a sign of the cross within its baptismal liturgy. For more about immersion, see our guide to believers’ baptism.

If you are asking about someone’s previous baptism, explain what took place and which church conducted it. The question then concerns an actual event. If your question is about a prayer habit, describe that habit instead. This distinction saves a good deal of confusion, especially when a conversation moves between Russian and English or involves people with different church backgrounds.

What to do when visiting

You can listen to the prayers and follow the explanation of the service without trying to reproduce every movement you notice. If a direction is unclear, quietly ask someone what is happening. A person beside you may be following a personal habit, so copying that person is not always a reliable guide to the congregation’s shared practice.

If you usually make the sign of the cross and want to ask about it, a plain question is enough: “This is part of my prayer practice; how is it understood here?” That opens a conversation about meaning. There is no need to begin by describing another tradition as mistaken or by asking a guest to defend an entire denomination. Ask about the action that interests you and give the other person room to explain.
