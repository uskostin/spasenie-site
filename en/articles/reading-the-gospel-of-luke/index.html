Reading Luke: its opening, encounters and continuation

Luke opens with a letter-like address to Theophilus and closes with the risen Jesus sending His followers out as witnesses. Between those points, Luke follows Jesus from Galilee to Jerusalem and pays close attention to what happens when He enters a synagogue, sits at a table, or stops to speak to someone on the road. Those settings help a first-time reader see how the episodes fit together.

What Luke tells Theophilus

Read the first four verses before moving into the birth stories. The writer acknowledges that others have put together accounts and that the events were handed down by people who witnessed them. He describes his own work of following the material carefully and arranging it for Theophilus, who has already received instruction. His stated aim is to give Theophilus confidence about what he has been taught. The introduction gives no biography of the recipient. It gives the reader a purpose for the book. Luke 1:1–4

Try reading the next two chapters with that purpose in mind. The accounts of John and Jesus appear alongside each other, and the people who speak in them help explain what the births mean. For example, Zechariah’s words near the end of chapter 1 connect the birth of his son with the work of preparing people for the Lord. Read his speech in its setting, after the account of the child’s naming, and notice where it moves from the story of one family to the hopes of a people. Luke 1:57–79

Hear the reading in Nazareth and stay for the response

At the synagogue in Nazareth, Jesus reads from Isaiah about good news for the poor and release for captives. He tells the listeners that the Scripture is fulfilled in their hearing. The conversation then turns to His hometown and to people helped in the days of Elijah and Elisha. By the end, the people in the synagogue drive Him out. Read the whole exchange before drawing a conclusion from its opening. The sequence lets you see what Jesus says about His work and how quickly the listeners’ response changes. Luke 4:16–30

A practical note for reading this scene: make one column for what Jesus says and another for how the listeners respond. Put their words and actions beside the lines that precede them. If you read with someone else, compare which moment each of you marked as the change in tone. You can point to different details and still agree on the order of events.

Follow the road toward Jerusalem

At Luke 9:51, Jesus sets His direction toward Jerusalem. In the very next scene, a Samaritan village refuses to receive Him. James and John want to call down fire, and Jesus rebukes them. Keep that turn in view when the book moves into teaching and encounters whose immediate setting may be a village or a house. The journey gives those scenes a destination even when a passage offers little detail about the route. Luke 9:51–56

Near Jerusalem, pause at the meeting with Zacchaeus in Jericho. He wants to see Jesus, but the crowd is in his way. When Jesus goes to stay at his house, the crowd objects to His choice of host. Pay attention to what Zacchaeus says about giving to the poor and making repayment, and then read how Jesus explains His visit. The scene moves from a man trying to see over a crowd to a public conversation about restitution and whom Jesus came to seek. It makes a useful comparison with Nazareth: start with what each group actually says before deciding what the reactions have in common. Luke 19:1–10

Read the ending and then the opening of Acts

On the road to Emmaus, two disciples tell a stranger what has happened in Jerusalem. Luke has already told the reader who the stranger is. That difference between what the reader knows and what the travelers know gives their conversation room to unfold. Jesus explains the Scriptures, and the two recognize Him at the meal. When they go back to Jerusalem, they have something to tell the others. Later in the chapter Jesus speaks of witnesses and tells His followers to wait in the city. Luke 24:13–49

Open Acts 1 immediately afterward. Theophilus appears again, and the opening refers back to a first book about what Jesus did and taught. The instruction to wait in Jerusalem and the call to bear witness carry into this new account. Write down the places named in Acts 1:8. They give you an idea of the widening scope of the story you are about to read. Acts 1:1–8

To keep track across several sittings, write one sentence about each scene before you close the book. Name the setting and the person who speaks or acts. When you return, read the last paragraph from the previous sitting before starting a new chapter. In Luke 24 this habit pays off: the road, the meal, and the return to Jerusalem each change who is present and what can be said about the encounter.
