How to start reading the Acts of the Apostles

Begin Acts by reading its opening beside the beginning and end of Luke’s Gospel. Both books address Theophilus, and Acts refers back to an earlier account of Jesus’ deeds and teaching. The new narrative follows his witnesses after the resurrection, beginning in Jerusalem and eventually reaching Rome. Acts 1:8 gives an early orientation through its words about the Holy Spirit and witness in widening geographical circles. Use that statement as a guide while following the people, speeches, and journeys in the chapters ahead.

Read the two introductions together

Luke 1:1–4 describes the purpose of an orderly account addressed to Theophilus. Acts 1:1–5 refers to a first book and resumes with the risen Jesus speaking to the apostles. Reading these openings together helps you recognize continuity before meeting the many names and places that appear later.

Then reread Luke 24:44–53. Its closing scenes include the Scriptures, witness, the promise from the Father, and the disciples in Jerusalem. Acts returns to these concerns. A first-time reader can therefore begin with a connection between the books rather than a disconnected list of events. Our overview of Luke provides a starting point if you have not yet read the Gospel.

Find the opening question and commission

In Acts 1, the gathered disciples ask Jesus about restoring the kingdom to Israel. His answer turns them away from knowing the Father’s times and toward the power they will receive and the witness they will give. The statement names Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, and the ends of the earth.

Write those places on a page and add later locations as you meet them. The exercise helps you follow a narrative that repeatedly moves between a gathering, a speech, a journey, and another encounter. Keep the commission beside the disciples’ question. It shows what Jesus directs their attention toward at the beginning of the book.

Start with Jerusalem and Pentecost

Acts 2 describes the coming of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, the crowd’s different reactions, and Peter’s public explanation. Read the speech with the event that prompts it. Listeners ask what is happening, and Peter responds by interpreting it through Scripture and speaking about Jesus.

For a first session, identify who speaks and who is listening before trying to explain every detail. A speech in Acts often answers a question or responds to a situation. Removing it from that setting makes it harder to see why a particular point is made. Continue to the end of the chapter, noticing how the account moves from the public gathering to the shared life of those who receive the message.

Follow people across boundaries

Acts 8 moves into Samaria and then follows Philip’s encounter with an Ethiopian traveler. Acts 10 brings Peter to the household of Cornelius. These are useful landmarks for readers because they show the story reaching people beyond its opening Jerusalem setting. Read each encounter in full before comparing them.

At each new location, note who initiates contact, what questions are asked, and what explanation is given. You do not need to memorize every place immediately. A simple reading log can hold the name of the place and the people involved. Return to Acts 1:8 from time to time and see how the movement of the narrative relates to its opening commission.

Notice when the journeys become prominent

Acts 13 opens with the community at Antioch and the sending of Barnabas and Saul. Journeys occupy much of the narrative that follows. For orientation, follow departures and arrivals rather than trying to create a complete travel map on your first reading. Add a location only when the passage actually names it.

The people and circumstances also change. Ask what happens at each stop and why the travelers leave. Sometimes the next scene depends on a disagreement, an invitation, or opposition described in the previous one. Keeping those links makes the journeys easier to read as a developing account instead of isolated stories about different cities.

Distinguish a recorded event from an instruction

Acts contains actions that the narrator reports, words addressed to particular listeners, and explanations that help readers understand what has happened. Those are related forms of writing, but they require careful attention. Begin by asking who is doing or saying something and to whom.

An event’s inclusion does not by itself establish a rule requiring every later congregation to repeat every detail. When considering application, look for an explicit instruction and its setting. Compare related passages before drawing a broad conclusion. You can bring an unresolved question to a discussion while keeping a clear distinction between what the chapter states and the interpretation you are considering.

A reading route you can adjust

For an opening round, read Luke’s introduction and conclusion, followed by Acts 1–2. In later sessions, take Acts 8 and Acts 10 as landmarks, then begin Acts 13 and continue through the journeys at a manageable pace. This is an orientation route; reading the intervening chapters in order gives the connections their full setting.

End by noticing where Acts 28 leaves Paul: in Rome, receiving visitors and speaking about God’s kingdom and Jesus Christ. The closing verses keep the proclamation in view. They do not supply a full account of everything that happens afterward. For each session, write one sentence about the scene you read and one question to carry forward. Our guide for beginning readers offers a simple note-taking approach that you can also use here. Return to the surrounding chapter when a sentence seems difficult to understand.
