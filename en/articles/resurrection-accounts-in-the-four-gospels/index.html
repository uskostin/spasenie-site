What do the four Gospels say about Jesus’ resurrection?

All four Gospels tell of women coming to Jesus’ tomb after the Sabbath and finding that the stone has been moved. Each account gives the reader a different vantage point. Matthew follows the women as they carry the news. Mark ends the first scene with their fear. Luke follows two travelers to Emmaus, and John stays with Mary Magdalene as she recognizes Jesus. Read each account through before comparing the details.

Matthew: from the tomb to Galilee

In Matthew 28, Mary Magdalene and the other Mary come to see the tomb. Matthew describes an earthquake, an angel moving the stone, and guards overcome by fear. The angel tells the women that Jesus has risen and sends them to tell his disciples. Their departure is marked by both fear and joy. On their way they meet Jesus, who also directs them to send the disciples to Galilee.

The meeting in Galilee comes at the end of the chapter. Notice how the destination announced at the tomb becomes the setting for Jesus’ instructions to the disciples. The chapter also records a competing explanation circulated by the guards. Matthew gives that report its own place in the story, alongside the women’s message and the meeting in Galilee. Keeping track of who speaks helps a reader distinguish what each person claims from what the narrator describes.

Mark: a message received with fear

In Mark 16:1–8, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome bring spices. They wonder who will move the stone. When they arrive, it has already been rolled away. Inside the tomb they see a young man in white. He announces that Jesus has risen and tells them to bring word to the disciples and Peter. He points them toward Galilee.

Verse 8 leaves the women trembling and afraid as they flee. This scene records the announcement and their first response to it. Some editions set verses 9–20 apart or add a note about the manuscripts. When comparing translations, check how your edition presents those verses. You can compare the scenes at the tomb using verses 1–8 and then read the longer ending separately, paying attention to what your edition says about it.

Luke: what the travelers come to understand

In Luke 24, the women find the stone moved and encounter two men in dazzling clothing. They are reminded of what Jesus said while he was still in Galilee. When they report what happened, the apostles initially dismiss their words. Luke then follows two disciples on their way to Emmaus. They know that the tomb was found empty and that the women brought a report. They are still trying to understand the events.

Jesus joins the travelers, listens to their account and explains the Scriptures to them. They recognize him when he breaks bread, and they return to Jerusalem. Read the conversation on the road alongside the later gathering in Jerusalem. Notice the different ways people come to understand what has happened. In both scenes, the Scriptures help the disciples make sense of Jesus’ suffering and resurrection. The return from Emmaus also changes the direction of the story. The travelers have something to tell the others.

John: recognizing Jesus by name

In John 20, Mary Magdalene sees the moved stone and runs to tell Peter and another disciple. They come to the tomb and see the linen cloths. After they leave, Mary remains outside weeping. When Jesus speaks to her, she takes him for the gardener. She recognizes him when he calls her by name and then carries news of the encounter to the disciples.

The chapter continues with the disciples gathered behind closed doors and later with Thomas, who was absent at the first gathering. Watch how John moves from what people see to what they hear from others. Mary’s initial report concerns the missing body. Her later report concerns meeting Jesus. Thomas hears the other disciples’ testimony before he has an encounter of his own. That sequence gives the closing words about belief and the purpose of the book a setting.

Compare the accounts on one page

Make four columns and write one Gospel name over each. For the opening scene, note who comes to the tomb, what they see and what they are asked to tell. In a second row, record where the account takes the reader next. Use a verse reference with each observation so you can check it later. If one writer mentions a detail and another leaves it unmentioned, record the wording of each before trying to fit the accounts into a single timeline.

Try the exercise with someone else and compare one observation from each column. Begin with a detail that surprised you. Matthew’s movement toward Galilee, Mark’s emphasis on fear, Luke’s return to Jerusalem and John’s attention to recognition each give the conversation a place to start.
