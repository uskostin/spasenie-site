How Baptists understand Christmas

Yes, Baptists celebrate Christmas. A recent example is the Baptist World Alliance’s 2025 Christmas message, which connects the season with the birth of Jesus and joy for people across cultures. To understand the celebration, begin with the Gospel accounts. A church’s decorations or family customs tell you much less than the passages being read.

What Christians remember at Christmas

Christmas concerns the birth of Jesus Christ. The opening chapters of Matthew and Luke place that birth within a larger account of God’s purposes. The story leads readers toward the identity and work of Jesus throughout the Gospels; it does not end with a picture of a baby.

The Southern Baptist Convention’s Baptist Faith and Message, section II.B, describes Jesus as the eternal Son of God who took human nature and was born of the virgin Mary. That document offers one named Baptist explanation of the incarnation. It helps explain why Christmas preaching can discuss who Jesus is, alongside the events surrounding His birth. Our article on Jesus Christ in Baptist belief explores that broader question.

Read Luke and Matthew separately first

Start with Luke 1, especially verses 26–38. Mary receives the announcement of the birth, asks a question and responds. Reading the conversation before moving to Bethlehem gives the familiar Christmas scene a beginning. Notice what the messenger says about the child, rather than reading only for details you recognize from a Christmas card.

In Luke 2:1–20, the account moves to Bethlehem, the birth and the shepherds. Notice the difference between what happens and how the people respond: hearing, going to see, telling others and pondering. Those responses give a family or reading group useful questions to discuss. Which detail prompted someone to act? What did Mary continue to consider?

Then read Matthew 1:18–25. Joseph’s situation and response come to the foreground. The explanation of Jesus’ name links His birth with saving His people from their sins. Matthew gives the reader a reason to look beyond the scene itself and ask what Jesus will do.

Matthew 2 introduces the visitors from the east and Herod. Read that chapter on its own terms before combining it with Luke. The text mentions three kinds of gifts, without specifying the number of visitors. It describes their arrival at a house. A familiar arrangement of figures around a manger can combine episodes that the Gospel writers narrate separately.

How to distinguish the text from a tradition

When a detail seems familiar, ask where you learned it. Was it in the passage, a song, a painting or a family story? Those sources can serve different purposes. A children’s picture may gather several episodes into one scene so they fit on a page. Reading the chapters separately lets you see the author’s own sequence.

A simple exercise is to divide a sheet into two columns. In the first, note a detail and its verse. In the second, write a question or a remembered custom. Leave uncertain points as questions. You do not need to settle the historical background of every image before understanding the main story, and you do not need to add missing details to make the account feel complete.

Why customs and programmes can differ

The Baptist World Alliance’s Christmas message is evidence of Baptists celebrating the season; it is not a programme for every congregation. A carol service, children’s presentation or shared meal should be confirmed through the church hosting it. The same applies to what a visitor should bring or whether a particular event requires registration.

Separate the subject of the celebration from its arrangement. Reading about the birth of Jesus answers what Christmas remembers. An event announcement answers when people are gathering and what they plan to do. A seasonal concert and a regular worship service can have different formats even when both concern Christmas. If you are inviting someone, send the announcement for the specific gathering.

Does the Bible give a Christmas date?

The birth narratives in Matthew 1–2 and Luke 1–2 do not name a day and month for Jesus’ birth. A celebration date therefore should not be presented as a date supplied by those chapters. The date printed on a church invitation tells you when that community will meet.

For visitors, the practical distinction matters. A church may announce a seasonal gathering separately from its usual weekly meetings. Check the event’s own date and starting time rather than assuming that a holiday name gives the whole schedule. Reading the story with children or for the first time

Choose one short passage for a sitting. Ask what happened, who spoke and what remains unclear. If a child asks whether a pictured detail is in the Bible, look together. An unanswered question can wait until the next reading. Our guide to starting a Gospel explains how to follow a passage in context.
