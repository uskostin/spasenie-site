Pastor, elder and ordination: what the terms mean

A Baptist church may describe a leader as a pastor or an elder. Ordination concerns a person’s recognition for ministry; it is a separate question from the title used in an introduction. To understand a church’s leadership, ask what responsibility the person holds and how the church recognised that ministry. The word “pastor” on its own will not tell you the date or circumstances of an ordination.

Pastor and elder: read the church’s own explanation

In Article VI of the Southern Baptist Convention’s Baptist Faith and Message, pastor, elder and overseer are grouped as names for one office, alongside the distinct office of deacon. This gives a concrete example of how a Baptist confession uses the terms. It is useful when you encounter different words in a church’s description and wonder whether they indicate different levels of authority.

When looking at another congregation, begin with its leadership page or constitution. Does it explain who teaches and who has responsibility for pastoral care? Does it identify a group of elders, a pastor or another arrangement? Read the description before assigning a meaning to the title. An English word on a website and its Russian translation may also need explanation, particularly if your previous church used a different vocabulary.

What ordination recognises

The Baptist Union of Victoria’s explanation of ordination describes recognition of a leader’s calling and preparation. It involves the local church and the union. This is a named Baptist body explaining its own process, and it illustrates why ordination is a matter of communal recognition rather than something a visitor can establish from a person’s speaking ability.

The practical distinction is between a role someone is carrying out and the process through which that ministry has been recognised. If you want to know whether a particular person is ordained, ask that question directly. A biography may provide the answer. If it does not, the missing sentence leaves the matter unanswered; it is not evidence that an ordination did or did not take place.

Why laying on of hands appears in the discussion

In Acts 13:1–3, the church at Antioch prays and fasts, lays hands on Barnabas and Saul and sends them out for the work to which they have been called. Read the whole passage: it describes a particular sending for ministry. The prayer and gesture belong to that setting.

This passage helps a reader understand why prayer and laying on of hands are discussed together. It does not supply every administrative step in a contemporary church’s ordination procedure. To learn about those steps, read that church’s explanation or the relevant Baptist body’s published guidance. Keeping the biblical account and the current procedure clearly identified makes both easier to discuss without claiming that every detail of one appears in the other.

Does an unordained pastor mean something is wrong?

The phrase “unordained pastor” needs context. It may be intended to distinguish someone’s present responsibilities from a formal act of recognition. Before drawing a conclusion, find out how the speaker is using both words. Ask whether the person has been appointed to a role, whether an ordination is being considered and who is responsible for explaining the arrangement.

These are questions to investigate, not a description of a particular minister’s circumstances. Avoid turning an unfamiliar phrase into a judgement about the person’s faith or character. Equally, a title should not prevent reasonable questions about responsibility. An explanation that identifies the church’s decision and the person’s actual duties will tell you more than an argument over what a title ought to imply.

Where deacons fit

The Southern Baptist statement names deacon separately from pastor/elder/overseer. When reading a church’s leadership information, therefore, do not automatically treat a deacon as a junior pastor or a step on a ladder toward becoming one. Look for a description of that church’s deacons and their responsibilities. The name of an office alone gives you less information than an explanation of the work attached to it.

For the wider question of how every member participates in church life, see our article on the priesthood of all believers. That subject concerns the whole congregation. Here the question is narrower: which responsibilities have been entrusted to a named leader, and how are those responsibilities recognised? Keeping the questions separate helps avoid making ordination the measure of every kind of service.

How to ask during a visit

You can begin with a straightforward question: “Who could explain how your church appoints its pastors and elders?” If you are asking about ordination, say so. Ask where the church’s written explanation can be read, and arrange a longer conversation if the person you approach is occupied. You do not need to frame the question as a challenge or assume there is a problem to uncover.
