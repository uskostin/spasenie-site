Why Jesus used parables and how to read them

Jesus’ parables call listeners to consider what they hear and how they respond. They use familiar scenes, yet the Gospels also record people failing to understand them and disciples asking for explanations. To read a parable carefully, begin with its setting, follow the story through, and include any explanation Jesus gives afterward.

The disciples asked the same question

In Matthew 13, the disciples ask why Jesus speaks to the people in parables. His answer addresses hearing and understanding, refers to Isaiah, and distinguishes the disciples’ reception from the failure to understand around them. Read the answer before reducing the purpose of parables to a single teaching technique. The passage places the listeners’ response at the centre of the question. Matthew 13:10–17

This matters when a story seems easy to remember. Familiar pictures can remain in the memory while the question they raise goes unanswered. For a first reading, ask what the people in the scene have heard and what happens next. Notice whether someone asks for an explanation. That question belongs to the Gospel’s account of teaching, and can guide your own next step.

Find the beginning and end of the encounter

Before reading, look a few verses back. Identify the speaker, the people addressed and the question or situation that leads into the story. Continue afterward until the response or explanation is complete. Printed section headings can help you find a passage, but a heading may separate an introduction from the story it introduces.

Luke 18 supplies a clear example. The narrator introduces one parable with a purpose concerning persistent prayer. Another is introduced by identifying people who trust their own righteousness and despise others. Those opening statements give direction to the reading. Preserve them when you retell either story. Luke 18:1–14

You could write the introduction above your notes and return to it after reading. Does your explanation address the situation the narrator names? If it answers a different question, reread the passage and identify how you reached that interpretation. This is a practical way to check a first impression before discussing it with someone else.

Use the explanation that accompanies the story

Mark 4 records the sower’s seed falling in different places, followed by a request about the parables and Jesus’ explanation. In that explanation the seed is connected with the word, and the different outcomes concern responses to hearing. The explanation gives the reader connections that can be checked in the text. Mark 4:1–20

Make two columns, one for what happens in the story and one for what the explanation says. Put related details beside each other. Leave an empty space where the passage supplies no separate interpretation. This keeps a memorable detail from becoming the starting point for an elaborate meaning that the passage never gives.

When discussing the story with children, first ask them to describe the scene. Then read the explanation together. Let them point to the connection in the passage. You can distinguish what the story says from an example you invent to help explain it by naming your example as your own illustration.

Compare accounts after hearing each one

Luke 8 also records the sower and an explanation. Its wording gives particular attention to holding the word and bearing fruit with perseverance. Read the account as it stands, then compare it with Mark. Record which details each passage actually includes. This gives the comparison a clear basis and helps you preserve the emphasis of each account. Luke 8:4–15

A notebook can help here. Put the references at the top of facing pages and summarize each account before combining observations. If a phrase appears in only one passage, keep its reference with it. When you return later, you will be able to find the wording without relying on a blended memory of several readings.

Move from observation to a question for yourself

After reading the complete encounter, state its main concern in one or two sentences. Then ask how it addresses your own hearing or conduct. Keep that application connected to the concern you have identified. An application is easier to discuss when another reader can see how you reached it from the passage.

For example, a reader working through the sower might notice a named pressure in the explanation and ask where that pressure affects their attention. That is an exercise in reflection. Use it with care in a group, allowing people to speak about their own circumstances. A story read together does not give a reader access to someone else’s private motives.

A manageable first discussion

Choose one passage, including its introduction and ending. Read it aloud, give everyone time to reread, and invite each person to identify one detail and one question. Open the text when answering. If a question remains unresolved, record it with the reference so that further reading begins from the same place.
