Why Jesus washed his disciples’ feet

Jesus washes his disciples’ feet in John 13 and then explains that he has given them an example to follow. Their Teacher and Lord has served them, and they are to serve one another. The conversation with Peter also shows that receiving what Jesus does comes before imitating it. Read John 13:1–17 as a whole: the action, Peter’s objection, Jesus’ answer, and his explanation belong together. Later in the chapter, the command to love one another gives the scene a wider setting.

The narrator introduces the action through love

The chapter begins before Passover, with Jesus knowing that his hour has come to depart to the Father. John speaks about his love for his own. The supper unfolds with betrayal already in view. These opening details mean that the washing takes place within a difficult and particular evening, as Jesus prepares his disciples for what is coming.

Notice how the narrator frames Jesus’ action with his knowledge of where he comes from and where he is going. The scene does not begin by saying he has forgotten his authority. That authority is part of the introduction. For a first reading, mark what John tells the reader before anyone at the table speaks. It prepares you to understand why the disciples’ Teacher takes up the towel.

Follow what Jesus actually does

Jesus rises from supper, lays aside his outer garments, takes a towel, and pours water into a basin. He washes the disciples’ feet and wipes them with the towel. John gives a sequence of ordinary physical actions that readers can follow without needing an imagined conversation among the other guests.

Stay with that sequence before moving to a moral lesson. What does the Teacher do, and what do the disciples receive? The direction of the action matters. Jesus himself initiates it. A short summary should include both the washing and the subsequent explanation; otherwise the reader may be left with a moving image but little sense of what Jesus says it means.

Why Peter objects

When Jesus reaches Simon Peter, Peter questions whether his Lord will wash his feet. Jesus says that Peter does not yet understand what he is doing but will understand later. Peter then refuses. Jesus answers that without his washing Peter has no part with him. Peter responds by asking for his hands and head as well.

This exchange makes Peter’s response more complex than a simple lesson in politeness. He must receive the action Jesus chooses to perform. Follow each reply in order, noticing how quickly Peter moves from refusal to asking for more. The Gospel lets us hear his difficulty and Jesus’ answer without requiring us to invent Peter’s unspoken motives. If discussing the story together, ask what changes between Peter’s first and final replies.

The words about being clean

Jesus answers Peter with language about bathing and washing the feet. He then says the disciples are clean, though not all of them. John supplies a specific explanation for that last qualification: Jesus knows who will betray him. Read the narrator’s explanation beside the words of Jesus rather than leaving the phrase detached from its setting.

The passage has prompted wider theological discussion about cleansing and the life of discipleship. Begin with the distinctions present in the scene itself: washing, receiving Jesus’ action, and the narrator’s reference to betrayal. A careful reading can acknowledge a question without claiming that one short sentence settles every later debate about Christian practice.

The Teacher explains the example

After washing their feet, Jesus puts on his garments and returns to his place. He asks whether the disciples understand what he has done. He accepts their titles of Teacher and Lord and makes those titles part of his reasoning: since he has washed their feet, they ought to wash one another’s feet. He explicitly calls his action an example.

The argument connects their treatment of one another with what they have received from him. Jesus then speaks about a servant and a messenger not being greater than the one who leads or sends them. Read those comparisons as part of the explanation. The question for the disciples is what conduct follows from belonging to this Teacher.

Knowing leads toward doing

Verse 17 connects knowing these things with doing them. It gives a practical direction to the conversation after the disciples have witnessed the action and heard the explanation. Understanding the scene includes considering the responsibility it places before them.

For personal reflection, choose an ordinary situation in which you can offer useful help. It might involve giving time to a task someone else is struggling to finish. Such a situation is an illustration for reflection, not an event claimed by this church or a replacement for the action described in the text. Ask whether the help answers another person’s need and whether you are willing to offer it without seeking attention.

Read on to the command about love

John 13 continues through the announcement of betrayal and Judas’ departure. Jesus later tells the disciples to love one another as he has loved them, and says their love will identify them as his disciples. Reading the whole chapter lets you place the example of service alongside that command.

Different Christian communities discuss how to express the foot-washing instruction in church practice. This article does not establish the practice of a particular congregation. Its immediate task is to follow Jesus’ own explanation in John. For your next reading, compare the example in verses 12–17 with the command in verses 34–35.
