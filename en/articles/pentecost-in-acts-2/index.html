Pentecost in Acts 2: what happens in the account

Acts 2 describes the disciples being filled with the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, people hearing them in their own languages, and Peter explaining what has happened. His speech leads to a question from the listeners, a call to repent and be baptized, and an account of the believers’ shared life. Read the whole chapter to follow that movement. The opening signs attract attention, while the speech and the response explain why the event matters within the story of Acts.

Begin with the promise in Acts 1

Before the event, Jesus tells the disciples to remain in Jerusalem and wait for the Father’s promise. Acts 1:8 connects the coming of the Holy Spirit with their role as witnesses. The opening of Acts 2 should be read beside that instruction. The people gathered at Pentecost enter a story whose direction has already been announced.

For a first session, read Acts 1:4–8 followed by Acts 2:1–13. Ask what the disciples have been told to expect and what the narrator now describes. This keeps the scene connected to Jesus’ commission. Our guide to reading Acts explains the book’s opening and offers a route through its later encounters and journeys.

What the opening verses actually describe

The gathered people hear a sound from heaven compared to a powerful wind. The text also describes divided tongues compared to fire, resting on each of them. They are filled with the Holy Spirit and begin speaking in other languages as the Spirit enables them. Keep the comparisons in your retelling: the narrator uses them to describe what is heard and seen.

A careful summary distinguishes the sound, the visible description, and the speaking. It need not turn them into a reconstructed scene with details about the building or people’s movements that the chapter does not supply. Read slowly enough to notice the narrator’s wording before asking how the crowd responds outside the gathering.

Why the listeners are surprised

Acts names people connected with many places and describes their amazement that Galilean speakers are heard in their native languages. They hear the mighty works of God being spoken about. The list of peoples and places belongs to the explanation of their surprise; the audience is able to understand what is being said.

The reactions differ. Some ask what this means, while others mock and suggest drunkenness. These responses give Peter’s speech its immediate setting. If you are reading together, let one person identify the question and another identify the accusation. Then read the beginning of Peter’s reply. That small comparison shows how the explanation answers the people in front of him.

Peter explains through Scripture

Peter stands with the eleven and addresses the gathering. He rejects the accusation of drunkenness, then identifies the event with what the prophet Joel spoke about the pouring out of God’s Spirit. The quoted passage includes different generations and servants, and ends with calling on the Lord’s name. Read the quotation as part of Peter’s answer to the crowd.

You can mark where the quotation begins and where Peter resumes addressing the listeners. This helps distinguish the Scripture he cites from his explanation of the present event. There is no need to understand every prophetic expression on a first reading. Write down a question, and continue far enough to hear the main direction of the speech.

The speech centers on Jesus

Peter goes on to speak of Jesus’ works, his crucifixion, and God raising him from death. He uses words attributed to David and explains how he understands them in relation to the resurrection. He identifies the apostles as witnesses and connects what the listeners now see and hear with the exalted Jesus and the promised Holy Spirit.

The speech therefore gives readers more than a description of unusual experiences. Peter directs the crowd toward Jesus and the meaning of his resurrection. Follow the progression of his argument, noting each reference to Jesus. If a verse is difficult, read it with the sentences around it rather than treating it as an isolated statement. The closing declaration in verse 36 prepares the listeners’ next question.

The listeners ask what to do

Verse 37 records a new question, addressed to Peter and the other apostles. Peter answers with a call to repent and be baptized in Jesus Christ’s name, speaking of forgiveness and the gift of the Holy Spirit. He also speaks of the promise extending to those whom the Lord calls. The narrator then describes people receiving the word and being baptized.

Keep that response connected to the message they have just heard. The chapter moves from wondering about the event to asking how to respond to the proclamation. A helpful discussion question is what changes between the question in verse 12 and the one in verse 37. Show where each appears before offering an interpretation of the change.

Continue to the life of the believers

The final verses describe devotion to the apostles’ teaching and fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer. They also describe sharing resources in response to need, gathering, meals, and praise. Stopping at the end of Peter’s speech would miss the life together that the narrator includes afterward.

For a second reading, divide the chapter into the event, the explanation, the response, and the closing description of community. These are reading aids, not separate stories. Write one sentence connecting each part to the next. The account itself does not establish how a particular congregation celebrates Pentecost today. If you want to continue with the wider narrative, return to the Acts reading guide and read the next chapter in sequence, carrying forward one question about what these witnesses say and do.
