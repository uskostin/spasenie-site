What is the Sermon on the Mount about?

The Sermon on the Mount is Jesus’ teaching in Matthew 5–7 about life before the Father and relationships with other people. It moves through the Beatitudes, the intentions behind conduct, prayer, possessions and the response to Jesus’ words. Its closing picture of two houses asks readers to connect hearing with doing.

Read the opening and ending together

Matthew introduces Jesus going up the mountain, sitting down and teaching as His disciples approach. At the end, the crowds respond with astonishment at His authority. These details frame the teaching as an encounter with listeners. Start by reading the opening verses and the final paragraph, then return to the beginning for a continuous reading. Matthew 5:1–2, 7:28–29

This helps when familiar sentences have been heard separately for years. You may recognize a saying about judging or a line from the Lord’s Prayer without remembering the neighboring paragraphs. Keep a question in your notebook about how each familiar passage contributes to the larger address. You can return to it after reading all three chapters.

The Beatitudes introduce the people addressed

The opening blessings speak of people who mourn, show mercy, make peace and hunger for righteousness, among others. Read the blessings with the promises attached to them. Notice the references to the Kingdom of Heaven near the beginning and end of the sequence. The following images of salt and light turn attention toward the disciples’ visible life among others. Matthew 5:3–16

For discussion, choose one blessing and paraphrase it carefully. Keep the person described and the promise together. A paraphrase is a way to check understanding, so compare it afterward with the wording of the passage. Let another reader ask where your explanation comes from. This gives the conversation a concrete basis.

Conduct and the heart belong in the same reading

Chapter 5 includes Jesus’ words about the law and prophets, followed by teaching that reaches into anger, desire, speech and treatment of enemies. Follow the movement from a command the listeners have heard to Jesus’ instruction. The passage about anger, for example, continues into reconciliation. Read that development before summarizing the subject in a single sentence. Matthew 5:17–48

A useful first question is what action the passage asks of the listener. You can then consider the attitude involved. Keep both observations beside the reference. Difficult sections deserve careful study in their full setting. If a particular sentence raises a question you cannot answer, write it down rather than making a personal decision from an isolated phrase.

Who sees the act of devotion?

The beginning of chapter 6 discusses giving, prayer and fasting. A repeated concern is the desire to be seen by others, alongside the Father’s knowledge of what is done in secret. The Lord’s Prayer appears within this teaching. Read it as part of the address to the Father and notice how the requests relate to God’s will and ordinary human needs. Matthew 6:1–18

Compare the teaching about light in chapter 5 with the warning about display here. In the first passage, the Father is glorified through the disciples’ works. In the second, the person acts to attract human recognition. Reading the passages together helps you ask what purpose the action serves in each setting.

Treasure and worry bring ordinary concerns into view

Later in chapter 6, Jesus speaks about treasure, competing service and anxiety over food and clothing. The instruction to seek God’s Kingdom appears within that sequence. Follow the argument through to its final words about tomorrow. The passage connects questions of trust and priority with needs listeners would recognize in daily life. Matthew 6:19–34

When reading together, ask which concern the passage names and what image Jesus uses in answering it. Keep discussion rooted in those details. Personal reflection can remain private, especially when someone is dealing with financial pressure. A group can study the passage attentively while giving people room to decide what they wish to share.

Chapter 7 leads toward a response

The final chapter addresses judging, asking the Father, treatment of others and discernment. Its picture of the speck and beam directs attention to examining oneself. Later, the images of fruit and the two builders ask listeners to consider what follows from words. Read the sequence carefully and note the references to action. Matthew 7:1–27

The two builders both hear. The difference described in the conclusion concerns doing what has been heard. Return to an earlier part of the sermon and ask how that ending affects your reading of it. You might choose the passage about reconciliation or the teaching about prayer and identify one question it raises for your conduct.

A plan for a first reading

Read one chapter at a time, recording an observation and an unresolved question. Then read chapters 5–7 together and revisit the notes.
