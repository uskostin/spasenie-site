Reading Mark: events and the disciples’ questions

Mark follows Jesus through encounters that keep raising a question: who is He, and what does it mean to follow Him? The disciples see His works but struggle to understand His suffering. Reading their questions alongside Jesus’ answers helps you follow the movement of the whole book.

Begin with the opening, rather than a theory about the author

Mark 1 introduces Jesus Christ and moves quickly through John’s ministry, Jesus’ baptism and temptation, and His preaching in Galilee. There is no birth narrative here. Notice what this opening chooses to place before the reader: Jesus announces God’s Kingdom and calls people to repent and believe.

Simon and Andrew are called while fishing; James and John leave their work too. Read the call together with the scenes that follow. Being near Jesus gives the disciples much to witness, but understanding takes time. You can observe that tension without first settling questions about the book’s date or the author’s sources. This guide follows the narrative itself.

Read actions together with the responses they provoke

For each encounter, make two short notes: what Jesus does, and how people respond. In the opening chapter His teaching and command over an unclean spirit provoke astonishment. Later, people can witness an event and still disagree about its meaning. Avoid treating every crowd as though it speaks with one voice or represents the same response throughout the book.

Keep the surrounding paragraphs attached to the passage. A healing, a conversation and a departure may belong to one sequence. If you stop at the most memorable sentence, you can miss the question the next scene raises. Section headings in your Bible help you find a passage; reading across them helps you see connections.

Listening and fear meet in chapter 4

Mark 4 puts teaching about hearing beside the disciples’ experience in a storm. In the parable of the sower, hearing has different outcomes. The explanation names pressures that prevent the word from bearing fruit, including trouble and the attraction of riches. Read the explanation before assigning a meaning to every detail yourself.

Later in the chapter the disciples wake Jesus in fear, and after the storm is calmed they ask about His identity. Their question remains significant even after they have witnessed His power. As a reading exercise, compare what they have seen with what they still cannot explain. Keep this observation about the scene separate from an assessment of another person’s faith.

Chapter 8 changes what the disciples must understand

In Mark 8:27–38, Peter identifies Jesus as the Christ. Jesus then speaks about rejection, death and rising again. Peter objects. The sequence matters: naming Jesus correctly does not mean Peter has accepted what Jesus says about His path.

Read the teaching about following Jesus immediately after that exchange. It links discipleship with self-denial and taking up the cross. Do not turn this into a prediction of the specific hardship any particular reader will experience. Ask instead how Jesus’ words challenge the expectations displayed in the conversation. Revisit this passage when you reach the arrest and crucifixion; the later events give the earlier announcement a concrete setting.

Watch how ambition enters the conversation

Mark 10:32–45 brings another announcement of suffering alongside James and John’s request for places of honour. Jesus addresses their request and the other disciples’ reaction. His answer describes greatness through service and speaks of His own life being given for others.

Notice whose expectations are being corrected before applying the passage. It is easy to make a list of the disciples’ mistakes while overlooking how familiar their concerns are: recognition, security, a place ahead of someone else. For discussion, choose one detail in the exchange and explain how the answer addresses it. A specific observation is more useful than a general claim that the disciples never understood anything.

Give the final chapters enough reading time

In Mark 15, follow the trial and crucifixion in their narrated order. Notice the repeated language about kingship, the mocking, and the centurion’s response at Jesus’ death. These scenes return to the identity question under circumstances very different from the earlier public ministry.

Continue into Mark 16:1–8, where the women visit the tomb and hear that Jesus has risen. Their fear is part of the account too. Some Bible editions include a note about the manuscript evidence for 16:9–20. Read your edition’s note rather than assuming a printing error. Questions about that passage deserve separate attention; they need not prevent a careful reading of the events and announcement in 16:1–8.

A practical way to read Mark as a whole

Use a notebook with space for a passage reference, a question raised in the scene, and the answer or response that follows. Leave room to revise an observation when a later chapter helps you understand it. Work through manageable sections, then reread chapters 8–10 together. This keeps the movement toward Jerusalem in view.
