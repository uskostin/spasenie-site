First visit to a Baptist church: questions to ask

A first visit can leave you with a question you did not know how to ask before the service. What did the preacher mean by faith? Why did people share bread and a cup? How does someone become a member? You can begin with one of those questions. A useful conversation does not require you to know Baptist vocabulary or have a decision ready.

Start with what brought you to church

Tell the person you speak with what you are hoping to understand. You may be visiting with a friend, returning to church after a long absence or hearing a Christian sermon for the first time. You can give that context in a sentence without describing everything about your life. It helps the other person choose a starting point instead of assuming that you already share their background.

A suggested opening is: “I am new here. I would like to understand how you explain this part of the service. Is there someone I could speak with afterward?” This is an example of a question, not a quotation from a church member. If the person you approach is busy, ask when a longer conversation would be convenient.

Ask how the church explains the Bible

The Baptist World Alliance’s beliefs statement identifies Scripture as authoritative for faith and conduct. For a visitor, that makes a question about a passage especially useful: which text was read, what is happening around it, and how does the explanation follow from it? You can ask for the reference and read it again at home.

Try to separate what the passage says from what you think you heard the speaker say. A sermon may move between a biblical story, an explanation and an illustration. If one sentence puzzles you, note its wording and ask which part of the passage it explains. That is more likely to produce a clear answer than a debate about whether the whole sermon was right or wrong.

Acts 17:10–12 describes people in Beroea who listened and examined the Scriptures. You can read that account as a prompt to listen carefully and check what you are learning. It does not turn one visitor’s interpretation into an automatic verdict on everyone else. A question can remain open while you study it.

Clarify what baptism means before discussing a date

If baptism comes up, ask what the church means by personal faith and what preparation it offers. The BWA statement describes believers’ baptism by immersion in connection with repentance and faith in Jesus Christ. Questions about your own situation should then be taken to the congregation: whom would you speak with, and what would the next conversation involve?

If you were baptized as a child or in another Christian tradition, describe that experience accurately and ask how the church understands it. You do not need to settle the issue during a brief conversation in a hallway. Our article on believers’ baptism gives background for the discussion. Bring the question that actually matters to you rather than trying to anticipate every possible disagreement.

Ask about the Lord’s Supper before taking part

If bread and a cup are shared during your visit, ask a leader to explain the meaning of the Lord’s Supper and the congregation’s guidance for participants. You can phrase it plainly: “I am visiting and do not know your practice. Who is invited to participate?” An explanation should address your question without requiring you to guess from what other people are doing.

Attending a service, understanding its teaching and participating in a particular church practice are separate steps. Ask beforehand when possible, especially if you are coming from a different tradition. For background, see our introduction to the Lord’s Supper. The local explanation is what you need for the service you are attending.

Learn what membership would involve

When you want to learn more about belonging to the congregation, ask how it distinguishes a visitor from a member. What beliefs are members asked to affirm? How are decisions made, and what responsibilities come with joining? These questions are about a considered commitment; an invitation to lunch or a friendly conversation does not answer them on its own.

Ask whether there is a written explanation you can read and whom you should approach with questions afterward. You can compare that information with our guide to Baptist church membership. Take note of the congregation’s actual answers rather than treating a general article as its application procedure.

Leave with a manageable next step

You might leave with a passage to read, a contact name or an agreed time to talk. Choose the next step that matches your question. If you heard an unfamiliar term, ask for an ordinary-language explanation. If you disagreed, restate what you understood and let the other person clarify before drawing a conclusion.
